How to Access Your Case Files Online

If you are a party to a case, you have the right to view the documents filed in your case. Every state court system has an online portal where you can look up your case, view filings, check hearing dates, and see what motions have been submitted. The name of the system varies by state — Maryland calls it "Party Access," other states call it Case Search, eCourts, Odyssey, or something else entirely.

Questions Answered in Episode 2

How do I see the documents filed in my own court case in Maryland?

Use the Application for Party Access, Form MDJ-004, from the Maryland Judiciary website. First create a free Case Search account. The email on that account has to match the email on your application. Take the form to the clerk's office with a valid government-issued photo ID. Once you are approved, the docket entries in Case Search become clickable, and you can open the actual filings, orders and notices. Other states call this something else: case access, party access, portal access or registered user access. Find your state on the map on this page.

Is a drug test cup proof that someone used drugs?

No. The cup is a presumptive test. It tells you something might be there. As the American Academy of Family Physicians puts it, all results are considered presumptive until confirmatory testing is performed, and positive results must be verified. The episode walks through common medications that have been reported to trigger false positives on these cups: ibuprofen and Benadryl reading as PCP, pseudoephedrine (Sudafed) and Wellbutrin can read as amphetamines, and some heartburn medicine can read as marijuana. A positive cup means one thing: send it to a lab.

What is a Public Information Act request?

It is Maryland's version of a FOIA request. It lets any person ask a government agency for public records. Every state has its own public records law.

Is staying silent enough to protect your rights during police questioning?

Not by itself. In Berghuis v. Thompkins (2010), a man sat through nearly three hours of questioning saying almost nothing, and the U.S. Supreme Court held that silence alone did not invoke his right to remain silent. You have to say it clearly: "I invoke my right to remain silent." Then actually stop talking.

How common are false confessions?

The Innocence Project reports that 29% of the DNA exonerations in its records involved a false confession. The episode says "roughly a quarter"; 29% is the Innocence Project's current figure. Innocent people are often the ones who talk, because they believe the truth will protect them.

What is a "technical" probation violation in Maryland?

Maryland defines it in the Correctional Services Article, § 6-101(m). It is a violation that does not involve an arrest or a summons on new charges, does not involve a criminal prohibition other than a minor traffic offense, does not involve a no-contact order, and is not absconding. It matters because a technical violation has a ceiling: 15 days the first time, 30 the second, 45 the third (Correctional Services § 6-223). A violation that is not technical has no ceiling.

This is legal information and my own experience, not legal advice. Laws and rules change and every state is different, so check the current rule yourself.
